Beneath Our Feet

An Introduction

Scroll down to travel beneath the surface — deeper into the ground, and further back through Earth's history.

02

Present Day

0 years ago · Surface

This is where your journey begins — right here on the ground you walk on every day, past the pavement and grass and topsoil that most people never think twice about. Beneath your feet lies a hidden world layered like the pages of a book: soil built from millions of years of decay, rock formed under unimaginable pressure, and traces of creatures that lived and died long before humans existed. Scroll down, and each stop takes you further from the present and deeper into Earth's past.

03

Soil

Forms over hundreds to thousands of years

Soil isn't just dirt — it's a living mixture of minerals, water, air, and organisms working together just beneath the surface. Earthworms tunnel through it constantly, aerating the ground and recycling dead plant matter into nutrients, while thread-like fungal networks called mycelium break down organic material and quietly connect plant roots to one another. Those roots anchor everything in place while drawing up water and nutrients from below, weathered mineral fragments of sand, silt, and clay give soil its texture, and a single teaspoon can hold millions of insects and bacteria breaking matter down. Over time, all of that decomposition becomes humus — the dark, nutrient-rich material that makes soil fertile.

04

Recent Past

~11,700 years ago and later

Animals from the more recent chapters of Earth's story — some still with us, some lost within human memory. The aurochs, wild ancestor of modern cattle, was hunted to extinction by the 1600s, while the dodo, a flightless bird native to Mauritius, disappeared within a century of human contact. New Zealand's giant moa once stood over three metres tall, and Steller's sea cow, a massive marine mammal, was wiped out within just 27 years of being discovered. The Irish elk, the largest deer ever to live, carried antlers spanning up to 3.5 metres — a reminder of how much has vanished in a relatively short span of time.

05

Ice Age

2.6 million – 11,700 years ago

Vast ice sheets shaped the landscape, and huge, cold-adapted mammals roamed the tundra. The woolly mammoth, covered in thick fur with enormous curved tusks, was built to withstand freezing temperatures, while Smilodon — the saber-toothed cat — hunted with dagger-like canine teeth. Shaggy-coated woolly rhinoceroses grazed the icy grasslands of Europe and Asia, and cave bears, larger than any bear alive today, denned throughout the region. Dire wolves hunted in powerful packs with heavier, stronger jaws than modern grey wolves, and giant ground sloths — some as large as elephants — browsed treetops with massive claws.

06

Mesozoic Era

252 – 66 million years ago

The age of dinosaurs — giant reptiles dominated the land, sea, and sky. Tyrannosaurus rex was one of the largest land predators to ever exist, with a bite force stronger than any land animal alive today, while the towering, long-necked Brachiosaurus browsed treetops no other dinosaur could reach. Triceratops carried three horns and a bony frill likely used for defence and display, and pterosaurs became the first vertebrates ever to achieve powered flight. In the seas, long-necked plesiosaurs hunted fish, and on land, the small, fast, feathered velociraptor was far smaller than its movie depictions suggest.

07

Paleozoic Era

541 – 252 million years ago

Life exploded in diversity — the first fish, insects, and land-dwellers appeared. Armoured trilobites thrived in the seas for nearly 270 million years, and Dunkleosteus, an armoured fish with bladed jaw plates instead of teeth, ranked among the era's top predators. Sea scorpions grew over two metres long, making them the largest arthropods ever to live, while Dimetrodon — a sail-backed animal often mistaken for a dinosaur — actually predates them entirely. Ichthyostega was one of the first four-limbed animals to venture out of the water and onto land, a small step that would eventually lead to all land-dwelling life.

08

Precambrian

4.6 billion – 541 million years ago

Earth's earliest chapter — simple life forms slowly gave rise to the first complex organisms. Stromatolites, layered structures built by cyanobacteria, remain among the oldest evidence of life on the planet, while Dickinsonia, a strange ribbed, oval organism, still leaves scientists debating whether it was an animal, a fungus, or something else entirely. Charnia, a frond-shaped organism anchored to the seafloor, was one of the earliest large life forms to exist, and Kimberella, a soft-bodied, slug-like creature, is thought to be one of the first animals ever to have a mouth.

09

The Mantle

~35 – 2,890 km deep

No life survives at these depths — but the mantle's slow-moving rock shapes everything happening above it. Temperatures here range from about 500°C near the crust to over 4,000°C near the core, and slow-moving convection currents of hot rock drive the plate tectonics that reshape entire continents over millions of years. Despite the heat, the mantle is technically solid rock — it just flows like an extremely thick liquid over long timescales. The immense pressure at these depths is also what forms natural diamonds, a process that takes billions of years.

10

Earth's Core

~2,890 – 6,371 km deep

The final stop on the journey — the heart of the planet itself. The inner core reaches around 5,400°C, roughly as hot as the surface of the sun, and it's surrounded by a liquid outer core made mostly of molten iron and nickel, constantly flowing and generating heat. That swirling molten iron is what produces Earth's magnetic field, an invisible shield that protects all life on the surface from harmful solar radiation. Despite the extreme heat, immense pressure keeps the inner core solid — a ball of iron roughly the size of the Moon.
